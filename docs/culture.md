# Batik tulis — what this game borrows, and how

This game is built around one real technique, and it tries to be accurate about
the part that matters: **the causal chain**. Everything else is deliberately
simplified for a four year old.

## The process the game models

`batik tulis` means *written batik* — batik drawn by hand with a **canting**,
as opposed to `batik cap`, which is stamped with a copper block.

The classic Central Javanese three-colour sequence, and the stage of the game
that corresponds to it:

| Javanese term | What happens | In the game |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **nglowong** | The first wax outline is drawn with a canting over the pattern. | `wax1` — the child traces the motif; this wax saves the **white** |
| **medel** | The cloth is dyed in indigo (*nila*). | `dip1` — swipe down into the blue tub |
| **mbironi** | Wax is applied over the blue areas that should stay blue. | `wax2` — this wax saves the **indigo** |
| **nyoga** | The cloth is dyed with *soga*, a brown from tree bark. | `dip2` — the brown tub |
| **nglorod** | The wax is boiled out; what it protected is finally revealed. | `lorod` — swipe across; the wax clouds, softens, flakes off |

A traditional workshop also uses `ngerok` (scraping wax back with a blunt
knife) between medel and mbironi, and repeats each dye bath many times over
days. The game collapses those repetitions into one continuous soak, but it
does not change the order or the logic — the reason a colour survives is always
"wax was covering it when the next bath happened."

That is the whole point of the game, and it is not a simplification: it is how
batik actually works. The lines the child draws first are the lines that are
*gone* at the end. What remains is the colour those lines were protecting.

## The canting

Three parts, all modelled in the scene:

- **cucuk** (also *carat*) — the fine copper spout the wax runs out of
- **nyamplungan** — the small copper bowl that holds the molten wax
- **gagang** — the bamboo or wooden handle

Copper is used for the bowl and spout because it conducts heat well and keeps
the wax (*malam*) liquid. In a real workshop the wax sits in a **wajan** over a
small stove (*anglo*) at roughly 60–70 °C.

**Safety.** The game never asks a child to handle anything hot. A craft robot
holds the canting and does all the dipping; the child's finger only guides its
hand. There is no burn, no spill, and no way to ruin the cloth.

## Motifs

The five motifs in the game are large, round, forgiving shapes drawn in the
*spirit* of well-known batik families — flowers (*sekar*), clouds (*mendung*),
water and birds, stars and leaves — plus a completely free mode.

They are deliberately **not** reproductions of specific named patterns.
Motifs such as **parang** and **kawung** carry real court, family and
ceremonial meaning in Java; *kawung* in particular was for a long time
restricted to the royal household. Handing a four year old a traced copy of one
as decoration would be careless, so the game does not do it. The motif families
are named in the picker (*sekar*, *mendung*, *banyu*, *lintang*) so a curious
grown-up has something true to look up.

The cloth itself is square, in the proportions of an **iket kepala** (a head
cloth), which is why it works equally well in portrait and landscape.

Indonesian batik was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the
Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009.
